# Thinking and story frameworks

The source cards in [the reference survey](../research/literature/survey.md) describe what was actually inspected and the limits of each framework. These are optional aids. None certifies a claim as true or requires every journal entry to end with advice.

| Writing situation | Thinking aid | Story shape | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A personal experience | Separate what happened, what you felt, what you now infer, and what remains open | One real scene → tension/question → reflection → an honest open ending or next step | Reflection inspired by Gibbs; publisher record only, R03 |
| An explanation or point of view | Reader question → tentative answer → grouped reasons → strongest challenge → revised answer | Orient the reader → explain the answer → evidence and qualifications → implication | Minto, R01 |
| A change or a project | Current situation → desired change → constraints and tradeoffs | What is → what could be → what it would take → a proportionate next step | Duarte, R02 |
| A researched essay | Question → competing explanations → inspected evidence → uncertainty → bounded conclusion | Question that matters → findings → counterevidence → interpretation → what remains unknown | SANRA-informed appraisal, R04; search reporting, R05 |
| A review or reading note | What the work argues → what supports it → where you disagree → who it might help | Specific encounter → useful idea → application or challenge → considered judgment | Project-authored synthesis |

## One-page thought brief

Before prose, write: the reader question; why you care; your provisional answer (or unresolved tension); 2–4 supporting reasons; evidence needed for each; the strongest alternative; what would change your view; and a boundary on the conclusion. These counts are editing defaults, not research findings.

Check for category errors: a memory is not a population study, an association is not a causal effect, a vendor claim is not an independent evaluation, and several articles repeating the same study are one evidence origin. Keep descriptive observations separate from advice.

## Preserve a personal voice

Use the author's supplied notes for scenes, dialogue, feelings and first-person assertions. If notes are missing, produce an outline or mark a clearly identified question for the author; never fill the gap with an invented life event. Preserve uncertainty, humor and unresolved questions when they belong to the material. Do not add drama by inventing conflict.

## Evidence before story

If evidence changes the conclusion, change the outline. Review headlines, openings and endings against the same ledger as the body. A good story cannot compensate for a misleading claim. Link the original framework and describe an adaptation in your own words; do not reproduce paid workbooks, figures or proprietary course materials.
