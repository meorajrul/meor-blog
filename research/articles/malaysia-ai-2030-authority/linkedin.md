Malaysia’s AI ambition needs a sharper question:

When a requirement is breached, who can actually compel action?

In July 2025, Bank Negara Malaysia announced a RM1.745 million penalty against Bank Islam for prolonged service disruptions. The notice identified the requirement, the breach and the legal power behind the penalty.

That was a technology-resilience case, not AI enforcement. But it gives us a concrete place to begin the AI Nation 2030 debate.

Before creating another management body, Malaysia should publish what its existing institutions can enforce—and where their powers fall short.

AI Malaysia already has stated coordination responsibilities. The national plan proposes central AI governance alongside sector regulators. Those responsibilities still need to be matched to the powers actually in force.

Three practical priorities deserve attention:

1. Publish the powers and name the owner. For each public AI service, identify the duty, its legal basis, who can obtain records, who can require correction and where a decision can be challenged.

2. Make supplier commitments enforceable. Show the acceptance criteria, record the findings and identify the official authorised to invoke the remedies in the signed agreement.

3. Give people a usable route to correction. A complaint contact is only part of the answer. Publish who can reconsider a decision, under what authority, and what happened to the cases reviewed.

A fine, a stopped breach and a corrected individual decision are different outcomes. Government reporting should say which occurred.

Another authority should make its case through evidence of a missing power, unresolved cases or a documented capability shortage—and explain why strengthening an existing institution would be insufficient.
