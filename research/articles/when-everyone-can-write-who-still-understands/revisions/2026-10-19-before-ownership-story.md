Imagine a project handoff: one AI writes the proposal, another summarizes it, yet someone still has to check whether the team agreed to a pilot or a full rollout. This is a hypothetical example, but it captures my concern: a completed document can leave the next decision unclear.

The question is whether the person receiving the output understands enough to act. Who owns the next step? Which requirement is current? What would change the decision?

Google Maps and Waze offer an analogy. Following directions can get us to a destination without ensuring we could explain the route or its alternatives. Navigation research gives this concern a measurable, narrower basis.

In [Dahmani and Bohbot's 2020 study](https://www.bic.mni.mcgill.ca/users/vero/PAPERS/Dahmani_Bohbot_GPS_Scientific_Reports2020.pdf), greater GPS use was associated with a steeper decline in spatial memory in a small follow-up: 13 drivers retested roughly three years later. The authors cautioned against strong conclusions.

A [2024 systematic review by Miola and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2024.102417) reviewed 23 studies and pooled data from 18. It found small negative relationships with environmental knowledge and self-reported sense of direction; wayfinding results were inconclusive. Most studies had moderate or high risk of bias. This does not establish general reasoning decline or a specific effect of Google Maps or Waze.

![Published pooled results: environmental knowledge r=-0.18, self-reported sense of direction r=-0.25, and inconclusive wayfinding r=0.07. Lines show 95% confidence intervals.](gps-navigation-research.png)

My connection to AI is a question: when we delegate the route through information, are we still building enough understanding to question the answer?

There are benefits to preserve. [Noy and Zhang report faster, better-rated writing in a bounded experiment](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/productivity-effects-generative-artificial-intelligence). AI can also help us examine a difficult passage. Reading every page unaided should not become the goal.

The same responsibility should apply to an AI agent. [Context-engineering guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) describes retrieving relevant material and compressing long conversations; excessive compression can lose important details. A summary still needs a route back to the source.

[AI skills package reusable instructions and resources](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills). A skill can tell an agent to verify scope, but the latest brief must establish what this customer actually requested. What feels like “laziness”—a skipped check or stale assumption—needs investigation before blaming context size.

For a useful handoff, I would ask the person or agent to:

- identify the current source and the constraint that matters;
- explain the decision and what remains uncertain;
- name the next action and its owner.

When requirements change or summaries conflict, return to the relevant original passage. An unchanged, verified copy can still be useful; repeatedly loading every file is unnecessary.

This brings me back to Iqra, the call to read in [Qur'an 96:1](https://quran.com/96/1). My application here is a personal reflection on engaging carefully with knowledge. [Hasrizal has also connected Iqra and AI with verification and accountability](https://hasrizal.substack.com/p/from-iqra-to-the-age-of-ai-what-al).

Before calling the work finished, can the person—or agent—point to the evidence and explain what should happen next?
