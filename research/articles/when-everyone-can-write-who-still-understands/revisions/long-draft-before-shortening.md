Imagine a project handoff. An AI assistant turns a brief into a polished proposal. The recipient asks another assistant to extract the action items. When someone asks whether the team is approving a small pilot or a full rollout, the answer still needs checking against the original requirement.

In this hypothetical example, the documents are complete. The shared understanding is unfinished.

That is the possibility that concerns me about AI-assisted work. We can make a document easier to produce without making the next decision easier to understand. The question I want to ask of a finished document is whether the person receiving it knows enough to act.

There is a useful name for one version of this problem. [BetterUp Labs describes “workslop”](https://www.betterup.com/workslop) as presentable AI output that lacks substance and leaves recipients doing additional interpretation or cleanup. Its account draws on a survey of US desk workers. That supports a bounded concern about recipient effort; it does not establish that people generally have stopped reading.

My question goes beyond poor-quality output. What happens if useful documents arrive faster than a team can examine their implications? A thoughtful proposal still needs attention. If the saved writing time becomes a reason to circulate more proposals, it is worth asking whether the team has gained capacity or simply moved the pressure to the reader. That is a possibility to investigate, rather than a measured result I can claim here.

There is also evidence for the benefit we should want to preserve. In [their account of a professional-writing experiment](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/productivity-effects-generative-artificial-intelligence), Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang report improvements in speed and assessed writing quality with ChatGPT. The tasks were bounded writing assignments. Their result does not answer whether recipients in a continuing project understood the work better, but it gives a reason to take the benefits seriously.

The distinction matters. Producing the document and receiving the document are different parts of the work. A writing improvement deserves credit. A handoff still needs to carry the information that lets someone decide, question or continue.

Navigation gives me an analogy. Imagine following Google Maps or Waze to a destination while paying little attention to the route. You might arrive successfully and still struggle to explain the alternatives or find your way without guidance. This is an analogy about how we use assistance, rather than a claim that navigation apps or AI inevitably damage memory.

An AI summary can be useful in the same way. It can get us to the part of a document that deserves our attention. The question becomes whether we can explain why that part matters, what assumptions support it, and what would change the decision. We do not need to memorize every turn to take responsibility for where we are going.

For a work document, I would look for a few things underneath the polished language. What decision is being requested? Which requirement is current? Which constraint rules out an attractive option? Who is expected to do something next? A summary that preserves those connections may be more helpful than a long original that leaves them obscure.

Reading, in this sense, involves checking the meaning we have taken from the material. It could mean opening a source passage, asking the author a precise question, or using AI to compare two versions and then examining the difference. The effort should follow the decision: a routine update and an irreversible commitment deserve different levels of scrutiny.

The same principle should apply inside a company that calls itself AI-first or AI-native. Whether a person or an agent prepares the next action, the current requirement still matters. General knowledge of an industry cannot settle what this particular customer requested or what this team most recently agreed.

[Anthropic's context-engineering guidance](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents) describes retrieving external material into an agent's active context. It also explains compaction: summarizing a long interaction to continue working. Compaction supports continuity, while excessive compression can discard important details. A stored source and a useful summary therefore serve different purposes.

For me, the practical question is whether the information needed for this decision is available and has been used. A reference somewhere in a repository is not an answer to that question. Neither is an assistant's confident assurance that it remembers the project. I would want to see the requirement that actually supports the proposed action.

AI skills offer a way to organize some of this work. [Anthropic's description of Agent Skills](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/equipping-agents-for-the-real-world-with-agent-skills) explains packaging instructions, scripts and resources that an agent can load as needed. A team could use that technique to document how a proposal should be checked.

The current proposal still needs its own evidence. In the imagined handoff, a skill might tell the agent to verify the scope before recommending a rollout. The agent would then need the latest brief to determine whether the customer authorized a pilot. Knowing the procedure and applying it to the current facts are separate responsibilities.

This is also why I would be careful with the word “lazy.” If an agent skips a source check or repeats an outdated assumption, the behavior deserves attention. Its cause needs investigation. A compressed detail, an unsuccessful retrieval, an unclear instruction or an outdated source would call for different corrections. A long conversation alone cannot tell us which occurred.

The standard I would propose is to keep the decision traceable. For a handoff that matters, the person sending the work should make it easy to identify:

- the decision or question being handed over;
- the relevant source and its current version;
- the constraints and uncertainties that affect the answer;
- the person responsible for the next action;
- the action itself, including when it is needed.

That is a proposed working habit, not a method I have tested. Its purpose is to make the gaps visible before someone acts. The recipient should be able to explain the next step in their own words and ask about anything the document leaves unresolved. An agent could help by locating the passages, comparing revisions or identifying a contradiction.

Returning to the source does not require reopening every file at every turn. A verified copy may still be current. But when the requirement changes, a summary omits a decisive condition, or two sources conflict, the original material needs attention again. Even then, opening it is only the beginning; the interpretation still needs checking.

There is a fair challenge to this argument: assistance may give people more room to think. A concise explanation can make difficult material approachable. A summary can direct attention toward the part that deserves deeper reading. Those are uses of AI I want this approach to make possible. Requiring everyone to work through every page unaided would miss that opportunity.

Nor should we assume that AI created every unclear handoff. Ambiguous ownership, unnecessary reports and poorly recorded decisions are alternative explanations to examine. If a team is struggling, the useful investigation is into the actual work: where did the requirement become unclear, what evidence was available, and what did the next person or agent do with it?

This brings me back to Iqra, the call to read at the opening of [Qur'an 96:1](https://quran.com/96/1). In the translation by Mustafa Khattab, the instruction is to read in the name of the Creator. The verse has its own religious setting; my connection to workplace AI is a personal reflection on the care with which we approach knowledge.

There is already thoughtful writing along this line. [Hasrizal connects Iqra and AI with questions of provenance, verification and accountability](https://hasrizal.substack.com/p/from-iqra-to-the-age-of-ai-what-al). The application I want to explore is the ordinary work decision: what do we need to engage with before we tell someone, or an agent, to proceed?

For me, a useful definition of finished work would include enough shared understanding for the next action to be explained and questioned. AI can help us reach that point. The document is part of the handoff; reading gives us a chance to check what we are actually handing over.
