I have worked in software since 2010. In the organisations I worked with, I watched desktop applications, database forms and Visual Basic for Applications (VBA) scripts give way to web-based systems.

Oracle Forms and Microsoft Access were part of that world. So were other desktop tools used to prepare documents, manage information and keep everyday work moving. Different tools served different purposes, but they belonged to the working environment I knew.

As companies and government bodies digitised their processes, the changes reached beyond the software.

Some tasks became redundant. Some people had to learn different skills or move into another role. New roles appeared around the systems being introduced.

That experience is where my question about AI begins. Having seen earlier changes in how work gets done, I want to understand what Malaysia should learn from them and where the comparison stops being useful.

## What changes when the tool changes?

In the company and government software projects I witnessed, moving work online also changed people’s responsibilities.

Some tasks became partly automated. People had to learn different skills or move into different roles, while new roles appeared around the systems.

These are observations from my working environment. They do not tell us how many jobs digitisation created or removed across Malaysia.

They do, however, give me a reason to ask more precise questions about AI.

Which tasks are changing? What happens to the people who used to perform them? Who receives the training and opportunity to move into the work that remains?

Counting how many people have access to an AI tool would answer only part of that.

## Is AI becoming the new PC?

I keep returning to a comparison with the personal computer.

Think of the difference between doing a task manually and having a desktop computer available to help with it. That is the comparison behind my concern: could declining to learn useful AI tools eventually put a worker at a similar disadvantage?

It is a question, rather than a conclusion that applies to every job.

Research already shows why the distinction matters. A study of 5,172 customer-support agents, with rollout mainly in late 2020 and early 2021, found that access to an AI assistant increased issues resolved per hour by about 15% on average. The gains differed across workers, with less experienced workers benefiting more. This was a particular tool in a particular company, rather than a result for the entire economy. [Generative AI at Work](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.11771v2)

Software development offers a useful caution. METR’s early-2025 experiment found that experienced open-source developers took longer on their tasks when AI tools were available. Its February 2026 update described later results as difficult to interpret because of participant selection and measurement problems. The researchers considered improvement plausible but could not confidently establish its size. [METR’s research update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

These findings leave room for both enthusiasm and scrutiny. Having a tool available does not establish that it improves a particular workflow. We need to look at completed work, its quality and the effort required to correct it.

Today’s AI already gives us work to examine. Whether artificial general intelligence (AGI), meaning AI with broad capabilities across tasks, will arrive, and what it would mean for work, remain separate questions.

## What does Malaysia’s next transition mean?

My starting question was about Malaysia’s movement from agriculture to manufacturing and towards services.

But I would be careful about extending that into a neat final stage called the “AI service economy.” Malaysia’s agriculture, manufacturing and services sectors continue to operate alongside one another. DOSM’s economic reporting describes their distinct contributions and performance. [DOSM, GDP Fourth Quarter 2025](https://storage.dosm.gov.my/gdp/gdp_2025-q4.pdf)

By “AI service economy,” I mean the possibility of AI changing how service work is done. I use it here as a question to investigate, without assuming it will become a separate stage of Malaysia’s development.

The more useful question may be what AI changes within the work Malaysia already does.

Does it improve the quality or value of a service? Does it help a business serve more customers? Do workers gain responsibilities and better pay, or lose tasks without a route into something else?

I cannot answer those questions from my software experience alone. They need evidence from Malaysian workplaces.

## What should we compare with Singapore?

Singapore is the comparison I naturally return to when thinking about Malaysia’s regional ambitions. But “catching Singapore” needs a clearer meaning.

Are we comparing business adoption, worker capability, service exports, or the ability of local companies to grow into regional businesses? Each would require different evidence.

My concern about government speed should face the same discipline.

Malaysia already has an AI agenda. The official AI Nation 2030 overview acknowledges that large-scale deployment across sectors remains incomplete, alongside gaps in advanced talent, investment mobilisation and research commercialisation. [AI Nation 2030](https://ai.gov.my/aiactionplan2030/)

That gives us something concrete to examine: what happens between a national commitment and everyday use?

Where a project is delayed, I would want to know the actual cause. Was it procurement, an unclear rule, unavailable data, limited skills, funding, or something specific to that organisation?

Calling all of this “government friction” would leave too much unexplained. Equally, the existence of a plan cannot tell us whether its delivery is working.

## What did we actually learn from Grab?

MyTeksi becoming a Singapore-headquartered company remains part of my concern about Malaysia’s ability to retain and grow businesses.

But the explanation deserves more care than saying regulation drove it away.

In a 2021 offering circular, Vertex described its investment in MyTeksi and said its general partner persuaded Anthony Tan to move the company’s legal home to Singapore. It also described help with regulatory introductions, taxi-company connections and executive recruitment. This is an investor’s retrospective account, with an interest in presenting its contribution positively. It nevertheless gives us several factors to investigate. [Vertex offering circular, printed page 147](https://links.sgx.com/FileOpen/Vertex%20Venture%20Holdings%20Ltd%20-%20OC%20dated%2012%20July%202021.ashx?App=Prospectus&FileID=52666)

It does not establish that one Malaysian rule caused the relocation, or that changing that rule would have kept the headquarters here.

The question I want to carry forward is what Malaysian founders actually need when they are ready to expand and whether they can obtain it here.

## Conclusion

My experience since 2010 has made me attentive to what happens around a change in software: the responsibilities that disappear, the people who adapt, and the work that takes a different form.

I want Malaysia’s AI discussion to make room for those details.

Learning to use AI matters to me, and I believe it matters to people entering the workforce, especially in the tech industry. So does finding out where it works, where it fails, and whether workers have a practical opportunity to adapt.

I would judge progress by what we can document in workplaces: better services, worthwhile businesses, and people finding a way into the changing work.

I hope that transition can be smoother, and that delays in regulation and policy do not hold back adoption.
