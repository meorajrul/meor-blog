A confident explanation can leave a quiet question unanswered: what would make this worth believing?

For this journal, a useful starting point is to separate the claim from the way it is presented. The question is not whether a page looks convincing, but whether its evidence supports the particular statement being made.

## Leave the page for a moment

Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew studied how fact-checkers, historians and students evaluated online information. Their work describes **lateral reading**: investigating a source beyond the page itself. The [original study](https://doi.org/10.1177/016146811912101102) offers a reason to ask who stands behind a claim before accepting its presentation.

In practical terms, that suggests opening the original report, checking who produced it, and finding out what other credible sources say. Treat this as an investigation, not a vote between search results. Several articles may repeat the same underlying report; their agreement alone does not provide several independent tests.

## Ask what was actually shown

An article might discuss a study without giving enough detail to judge the conclusion. What was measured? In whom? Compared with what? Does the result fit the question at hand?

[SANRA](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-019-0064-8), a tool developed for medical narrative reviews, includes attention to purpose, searches, references, reasoning and relevant data. Its setting matters. It does not validate this blog's editorial process. It does provide useful prompts for examining a researched explanation.

For a personal journal, the corresponding distinction is simple: an experience can be meaningful without proving a general rule. An observation, an interpretation and a recommendation each ask something different of the reader. The wording should make those differences visible.

## Leave a trail someone else can follow

A source link is a beginning. A section name, table number, date or passage locator makes it clearer what the link is supposed to support.

[PRISMA-S](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-020-01542-z) provides guidance for reporting literature searches. This journal borrows a small part of that practice: record where a search happened, what was searched and why a source was selected. That record does not turn an essay into a systematic review. It makes its research choices easier to examine.

## Keep the conclusion proportionate

The working approach here is to ask three questions: what is the exact claim, what supports it, and what would change the conclusion? When the evidence falls short, the options are to narrow the wording, state the uncertainty, or leave the question open.

This is a proposed editorial habit, not a guarantee of accuracy. It will be more useful to inspect its failures during real writing than to award it a confidence score before use.
